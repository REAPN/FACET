Diagnostic Criteria
===================

A. Lasting difficulty with social communication and social interaction that shows in more than one setting, now or in the past, through all three items below (the examples illustrate each item and do not exhaust it):
1. Trouble with the give and take of social and emotional exchange. Examples run from odd ways of approaching others and conversation that does not go back and forth; through sharing interests, feelings or emotional states less than others do; to not starting social contact or not answering it.
2. Trouble with the nonverbal behavior people use to communicate socially. Examples run from speech and nonverbal signals that fit poorly together; through unusual eye contact or body posture, or difficulty reading and making gestures; to no facial expression and no nonverbal communication at all.
3. Trouble building, keeping and making sense of relationships. Examples run from difficulty fitting one's behavior to different social situations; through difficulty joining others in pretend play or forming friendships; to showing no interest in peers.

B. Narrow, repeated patterns in behavior, interests or activities, now or in the past, through at least two items below (the examples illustrate each item and do not exhaust it):
1. Movements, handling of objects or speech that are stereotyped or repeated (for example simple repeated motor acts, arranging toys in rows or turning objects over and over, repeating the words of others, or phrases with a private meaning).
2. A need for things to stay the same, routines kept without flexibility, or ritual patterns of speech or nonverbal behavior (for example great upset at minor changes, trouble moving from one activity to the next, rigid ways of thinking, fixed greeting rituals, or needing the same route or the same food every day).
3. Interests that are very narrow and fixed and unusual in how strong or how focused they are (for example intense attachment to, or absorption in, unusual objects, or interests that are overly confined or perseverative).
4. Over- or under-response to sensory stimulation, or an unusual interest in the sensory side of the surroundings (for example seeming not to notice pain, heat or cold, strong aversion to particular sounds or textures, smelling or touching objects to excess, or fascination with watching lights or movement).

C. The signs are present early in development, though they may not show fully until social demands outgrow the person's capacities, and later in life they may be hidden by learned strategies.
D. The signs cause clinically meaningful impairment in social, work or other important parts of present functioning.
E. Intellectual disability or global developmental delay does not account better for these problems. The two conditions often occur together. Both diagnoses are given when social communication falls below what the person's general developmental level would predict.

Specifiers
==========
With or without intellectual impairment alongside the autism.
With or without language impairment alongside the autism.
Linked to a known medical or genetic condition or to an environmental factor.
Linked to another neurodevelopmental, mental or behavioral disorder.
With catatonia.

Diagnostic Features
===================
Verbal and nonverbal abilities should be estimated separately, for example with untimed nonverbal tests when language is limited.

Severity levels for autism spectrum disorder

Level 3, needing very substantial support.
Social communication: severe shortfalls in spoken and nonverbal social communication cause severe impairment, with very little starting of social interaction and very little response when others make social approaches. For example, a person with few understandable words who seldom starts an interaction and who, when they do, approaches others in unusual ways only to get needs met, and reacts only to very direct approaches.
Restricted, repetitive behaviors: inflexible behavior, great difficulty dealing with change, or other narrow or repeated behaviors interfere strongly with functioning everywhere. Shifting focus or action brings great distress or difficulty.

Level 2, needing substantial support.
Social communication: marked shortfalls in spoken and nonverbal social communication; social impairment visible even with supports in place; little starting of social interaction; and reduced or unusual responses when others make social approaches. For example, a person who speaks in simple sentences, whose interaction is confined to narrow special interests, and whose nonverbal communication is clearly odd.
Restricted, repetitive behaviors: inflexible behavior, difficulty dealing with change, or other narrow or repeated behaviors occur often enough for a casual observer to notice them and interfere with functioning in several settings. Shifting focus or action brings distress or difficulty.

Level 1, needing support.
Social communication: without supports, shortfalls in social communication cause noticeable impairment. The person has difficulty starting social interactions, responds to the social approaches of others in clearly unusual or unsuccessful ways, and may seem less interested in social interaction. For example, a person who speaks in full sentences and takes part in communication, but whose back and forth conversation breaks down and whose attempts at friendship are odd and usually fail.
Restricted, repetitive behaviors: inflexible behavior interferes markedly with functioning in one or more settings. Switching between activities is hard. Poor organization and planning limit independence.

For the language specifier, the current level of speech should be assessed and described. With language impairment may mean no understandable speech at all (nonverbal), speech of single words only, or speech in short phrases. Without language impairment may mean speaking in full sentences or fluently. In autism, understanding of language can lag behind its production, so receptive and expressive skills should be judged separately.
The specifier for a known medical, genetic or environmental link applies when the person has a known genetic disorder (for example Rett syndrome, fragile X syndrome or Down syndrome), a medical disorder (for example epilepsy), or a history of an environmental exposure (for example valproate, fetal alcohol exposure, or very low birth weight). Other neurodevelopmental, mental or behavioral conditions should also be recorded.
