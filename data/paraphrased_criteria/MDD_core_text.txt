Diagnostic Criteria
===================

A. Within one two-week period, five or more of the symptoms below are present and are a change from how the person functioned before. One of them must be item 1 (depressed mood) or item 2 (loss of interest or pleasure). Symptoms clearly due to another medical condition are not counted.
1. Low mood for most of each day on most days, either reported by the person (for example feeling sad, empty or hopeless) or seen by others (for example looking tearful). In children and adolescents the mood can be irritable instead.
2. Much less interest in, or enjoyment of, nearly every activity, for most of each day on most days, by the person's own account or as others observe it.
3. Marked weight loss without dieting, or weight gain (for example more than five percent of body weight within a month), or an appetite that is lower or higher on most days. In children, not gaining weight as expected also counts.
4. Sleeping too little (insomnia) or too much (hypersomnia) on most days.
5. Agitation or slowing of movement on most days, visible to others and not only a private sense of restlessness or of being slowed.
6. Tiredness or lack of energy on most days.
7. Feeling worthless, or guilt that is excessive or unwarranted (possibly to a delusional degree), on most days, beyond simply blaming oneself or feeling guilty about being ill.
8. Reduced capacity to think clearly or to concentrate, or trouble making decisions, on most days, by the person's own account or as others observe it.
9. Repeated thoughts about death (more than fear of dying), repeated thoughts of suicide without a specific plan, a specific plan for suicide, or a suicide attempt.
B. The symptoms cause clinically meaningful distress, or impairment in social life, at work or in another important area of life.
C. Neither a substance, through its effects on the body, nor another medical condition accounts for the episode.
D. Schizoaffective disorder does not account better for at least one major depressive episode, and the episode is not layered on top of a schizophrenia spectrum or other psychotic disorder (such as schizophrenia, schizophreniform disorder or delusional disorder).
E. There has never been a manic or hypomanic episode, unless every such episode was caused by a substance or by another medical condition.

Diagnostic Features
===================
Major depressive disorder requires one or more major depressive episodes and no manic or hypomanic episode at any time. The core of an episode is a period of at least two weeks with low mood, or with much less interest in or enjoyment of nearly every activity, for most of each day on most days (Criterion A). At least four further symptoms must occur in the same two weeks, drawn from: altered appetite or weight, altered sleep, and altered psychomotor activity; lower energy; a sense of worthlessness or guilt; trouble thinking, concentrating or deciding; and thoughts of death, suicidal thoughts, a suicide attempt or a plan for suicide. A symptom counts only if it is new or clearly worse than before the episode, and symptoms must be present on most days for at least two weeks in a row, except that thoughts of death and suicidal thoughts need only recur, and an attempt or a plan need happen only once. The episode must bring clinically meaningful distress or impairment in social, work or other important areas. In milder episodes functioning can look normal but takes much more effort. People often come with complaints of poor sleep or fatigue rather than low mood or lost interest, so other depressive symptoms can be missed if they are not asked about. Fatigue and disturbed sleep are very common. Psychomotor disturbances are much rarer but point to a more severe illness, as does guilt that is delusional or close to it.
People often call the mood low, sad, hopeless, discouraged or depressed (Criterion A1). A person may at first say they are not sad, and the sadness may only emerge during the interview, for example when the person is told they look close to tears. In people who say they feel nothing, feel flat or feel anxious, depressed mood may be inferred from facial expression and manner. Some stress bodily complaints, such as aches and pains, instead of sadness. Many report or show more irritability, for example lasting anger, a tendency to react with angry outbursts or to blame others, or excessive frustration over small things. Children and adolescents may become irritable or cranky rather than sad, which should be told apart from irritability that appears only when they are frustrated.
Some loss of interest in, or enjoyment of, ordinary activities is nearly always found. People may say they care less about hobbies, no longer care, or no longer enjoy what they used to enjoy (Criterion A2). Relatives often notice social withdrawal or dropped pastimes (for example a keen golfer who stops playing, or a child who loved football and now avoids practice). Some people have clearly less sexual interest or desire than before.
Appetite may fall or rise. Some depressed people have to make themselves eat; others eat more and crave particular foods, such as sweets or other carbohydrates. Severe appetite change in either direction can bring marked weight loss or gain, and in children a failure to gain weight as expected (Criterion A3).
Sleep may be too little or too much (Criterion A4). Insomnia usually means waking in the night and struggling to fall asleep again (middle insomnia), or waking too early without getting back to sleep (terminal insomnia). Trouble falling asleep at the start of the night (initial insomnia) can also occur. People who oversleep (hypersomnia) may sleep for long stretches at night or more during the day. Poor sleep is sometimes the reason the person seeks help.
Psychomotor change means either agitation (for example being unable to sit still, pacing, wringing the hands, or pulling or rubbing the skin, clothes or other things) or slowing (for example slow speech, thought and movement, longer pauses before replying, and speech that is quieter, flatter, sparser or narrower in content, or no speech at all) (Criterion A5). The change must be marked enough for others to see, not only a private feeling. People with one kind of psychomotor disturbance have often had the other.
Tiredness, fatigue and a lack of energy are frequent (Criterion A6). A person may describe lasting fatigue without exertion, find that even small tasks take great effort, and get things done less efficiently, for example finding that getting washed and dressed each morning is exhausting and takes twice as long. Much of the disability of the disorder comes from this symptom, in acute episodes and in incomplete remission.
Worthlessness or guilt may take the form of unrealistic negative judgments of one's own worth, or guilty brooding over small past failures (Criterion A7). People often take neutral or trivial everyday events as proof of personal defects and feel overly responsible for bad events. This can reach a delusional degree (for example being sure that one has personally caused poverty around the world). Blaming oneself for being ill, and for letting down work or relationships while depressed, is frequent and, short of delusion, does not by itself meet this criterion.
Many people say they cannot think, concentrate or make even small decisions (Criterion A8). They may seem easily distracted or complain of poor memory, and those in mentally demanding work often cannot cope. In children, poor concentration may show as a sudden drop in school marks. In older people memory complaints may dominate and be mistaken for early dementia (pseudodementia). These often clear when the depression is treated, though in some older people a depressive episode is the first sign of a dementia that will not reverse.
Thoughts of death, suicidal thoughts and suicide attempts are common (Criterion A9). They run from a passive wish not to wake up, or a conviction that everyone would do better if one were gone, through brief but recurring thoughts of suicide, to a specific plan. People at greater risk may have settled their affairs, obtained the means, and chosen a place and time. Motives can include wanting to stop trying when obstacles seem impossible to overcome, wanting to end an emotional state felt as endless and unbearably painful, being unable to imagine any future enjoyment, or not wanting to be a burden to others.
