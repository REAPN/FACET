Diagnostic Criteria
===================

A. At least two of the five items below, each present for a substantial part of a one-month period (or for less time if treatment succeeded). One of the two must be delusions, hallucinations or disorganized speech:
1. Delusions.
2. Hallucinations.
3. Speech that is disorganized (for example often going off track, or incoherent).
4. Behavior that is grossly disorganized, or behavior that is catatonic.
5. Negative symptoms, that is, reduced expression of emotion or loss of drive (avolition).
B. For a substantial part of the time since the illness began, functioning in at least one major area of life, for example work, relationships with other people or looking after oneself, is clearly below the level reached before onset (or, if the illness began in childhood or adolescence, the person never reaches the interpersonal, school or work functioning expected of them).
C. Signs of the illness last without a break for six months or more. The six months include at least one month of Criterion A symptoms (or less if treated successfully) and may include prodromal or residual periods, during which the illness may show only as negative symptoms or as two or more Criterion A symptoms in a weakened form (for example odd beliefs or unusual perceptual experiences).
D. The picture is not better explained by schizoaffective disorder, or by a depressive or bipolar disorder that has psychotic features, because either no major depressive or manic episode has coincided with the active-phase symptoms, or any mood episodes during active symptoms have taken up only a small part of the whole active and residual course.
E. The illness is not caused by the physiological effects of a substance (such as a drug of abuse or a medication) or by another medical condition.
F. If the person has a history of a communication disorder starting in childhood, or of autism spectrum disorder, schizophrenia is diagnosed in addition only if marked delusions or hallucinations are also present, along with the other required symptoms, for at least one month (or less if treated successfully).

Diagnostic Features
===================
Schizophrenia involves many kinds of problems of cognition, behavior and emotion, and no single symptom proves the diagnosis. The diagnosis rests on recognizing a cluster of signs and symptoms together with impaired work or social functioning, and people with the disorder differ widely on most features.
Two or more of the Criterion A symptoms have to be present for much of a month or longer, and one of them must be clearly present delusions (A1), hallucinations (A2) or disorganized speech (A3). Behavior that is grossly disorganized or catatonic (A4) and negative symptoms (A5) may also occur. If the active symptoms resolve within a month because of treatment, Criterion A still counts when the clinician judges that they would otherwise have lasted.
Functioning is impaired in one or more major areas (Criterion B). When it begins in childhood or adolescence, the person falls short of the level expected for their age, and comparison with unaffected siblings can help. The dysfunction lasts for a long part of the illness and is not simply the result of any one symptom. Avolition, a reduced drive to pursue goals (A5), is tied to the social dysfunction of Criterion B, and cognitive impairment is also closely tied to functional impairment.
Some signs must last without a break for at least six months (Criterion C). The active phase is often preceded by prodromal symptoms and may be followed by residual ones, with mild or subthreshold hallucinations or delusions. People may hold odd or unusual beliefs that fall short of delusions (for example magical thinking or ideas of reference), have unusual perceptual experiences (for example sensing that an unseen person is present), speak in a way that can be followed but is vague, and behave unusually without gross disorganization (for example mumbling in public). Before and after the active phase, negative symptoms are frequent and can be severe, and people who used to be socially active may withdraw from their usual routines. This is often the first sign of the illness.
Mood symptoms, and complete mood episodes, are frequent in schizophrenia and may coincide with active-phase symptoms. Unlike a psychotic mood disorder, schizophrenia requires delusions or hallucinations when no mood episode is present, and mood episodes must cover only a small part of the whole active and residual course.
Besides the five symptom domains of the criteria, cognition, depression and mania should be assessed in order to tell the schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders apart.

Associated Features Supporting Diagnosis
========================================
People with schizophrenia may show affect that does not fit the situation (for example laughing with no fitting reason), an unhappy mood, for instance depressed, anxious or angry; disturbed sleep (for example sleeping by day and being active at night); and little interest in eating, or refusal of food. Depersonalization, derealization and bodily concerns may occur and can become delusional. Anxiety and phobias are frequent.
Cognitive deficits are frequent and closely tied to impairment at work and in everyday life. They can include weaker declarative memory, working memory, language and other executive functions, and slower processing. Problems with sensory processing and with inhibitory control, and reduced attention, are also seen. Some people have deficits of social cognition, including trouble inferring what other people intend (theory of mind), and may notice irrelevant events or stimuli and read meaning into them, which may give rise to explanatory delusions. These problems often persist when symptoms remit.
Some people with psychosis lack insight into their illness (anosognosia): they are unaware of their symptoms, possibly throughout the illness. This unawareness is usually part of the illness rather than a way of coping, much like unawareness of neurological deficits after brain damage. It is the strongest predictor of not keeping to treatment, and it predicts more relapses, more treatment given against the person's will, worse social and psychological functioning, aggression and a worse course.
Hostility and aggression can occur, though spontaneous or random assault is rare. Aggression is more frequent in younger men and in people with past violence, poor treatment adherence, substance misuse or impulsivity. Most people with the illness are not aggressive, and they are more often victims of violence than people in the general population.
No imaging, laboratory or psychometric test currently establishes the diagnosis. Group studies using imaging, neuropathology and neurophysiology find differences in many brain regions between people with schizophrenia and healthy people, including cell architecture, white matter connections and gray matter volume in areas for example the temporal and prefrontal cortex. Total brain volume is reduced, and the loss of brain volume with age is greater than in healthy people. Eye tracking and electrophysiological measures also differ.
Common neurological soft signs include poor motor coordination, poor sensory integration, poor sequencing of complex movements, confusion of left and right, and disinhibited associated movements. Small physical anomalies of the limbs and face can also be present.
