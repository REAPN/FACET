Diagnostic Criteria
===================

A. Lasting difficulty with social communication and social interaction that shows in more than one setting, now or in the past, through all three items below (the examples illustrate each item and do not exhaust it):
1. Trouble with the give and take of social and emotional exchange. Examples run from odd ways of approaching others and conversation that does not go back and forth; through sharing interests, feelings or emotional states less than others do; to not starting social contact or not answering it.
2. Trouble with the nonverbal behavior people use to communicate socially. Examples run from speech and nonverbal signals that fit poorly together; through unusual eye contact or body posture, or difficulty reading and making gestures; to no facial expression and no nonverbal communication at all.
3. Trouble building, keeping and making sense of relationships. Examples run from difficulty fitting one's behavior to different social situations; through difficulty joining others in pretend play or forming friendships; to showing no interest in peers.

B. Narrow, repeated patterns in behavior, interests or activities, now or in the past, through at least two items below (the examples illustrate each item and do not exhaust it):
1. Movements, handling of objects or speech that are stereotyped or repeated (for example simple repeated motor acts, arranging toys in rows or turning objects over and over, repeating the words of others, or phrases with a private meaning).
2. A need for things to stay the same, routines kept without flexibility, or ritual patterns of speech or nonverbal behavior (for example great upset at minor changes, trouble moving from one activity to the next, rigid ways of thinking, fixed greeting rituals, or needing the same route or the same food every day).
3. Interests that are very narrow and fixed and unusual in how strong or how focused they are (for example intense attachment to, or absorption in, unusual objects, or interests that are overly confined or perseverative).
4. Over- or under-response to sensory stimulation, or an unusual interest in the sensory side of the surroundings (for example seeming not to notice pain, heat or cold, strong aversion to particular sounds or textures, smelling or touching objects to excess, or fascination with watching lights or movement).

C. The signs are present early in development, though they may not show fully until social demands outgrow the person's capacities, and later in life they may be hidden by learned strategies.
D. The signs cause clinically meaningful impairment in social, work or other important parts of present functioning.
E. Intellectual disability or global developmental delay does not account better for these problems. The two conditions often occur together. Both diagnoses are given when social communication falls below what the person's general developmental level would predict.
