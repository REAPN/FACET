Diagnostic Criteria
===================

Intellectual developmental disorder (intellectual disability) begins during development and involves shortfalls in both intellectual and adaptive functioning across the conceptual, social and practical domains. All three criteria below are required:
A. Shortfalls in intellectual abilities, for example reasoning, solving problems, planning, thinking in the abstract, judgment, learning in school and learning from experience, shown both by clinical assessment and by intelligence tests that are standardized and given to the person individually.
B. Shortfalls in adaptive functioning, so that the person does not meet the developmental and sociocultural expectations for independence and social responsibility at their age. Without continuing support, these shortfalls restrict functioning in one or more everyday activities, for example communicating, taking part in social life and living independently, in more than one setting, for example at home, at school, at work and in the community.
C. The intellectual and adaptive shortfalls begin during the developmental period.

Diagnostic Features
===================
The defining features are shortfalls in general mental abilities (Criterion A) and impaired everyday adaptive functioning compared with peers of the same age, gender and sociocultural background (Criterion B), beginning during development (Criterion C). The diagnosis rests on clinical assessment together with standardized testing of intellectual, neuropsychological and adaptive functioning.
Criterion A concerns intellectual abilities such as reasoning, solving problems, planning, thinking in the abstract, judgment, learning from teaching and from experience, and practical understanding. Its key parts include verbal understanding, reasoning with numbers, reasoning from what is perceived, working memory, abstract thought, and how efficiently thinking proceeds. Intellectual functioning is usually measured with valid, culturally suitable intelligence tests given to the person individually, on which people with the disorder score roughly two or more standard deviations under the mean of the population, allowing for measurement error. Clinical judgment is needed to interpret the scores, which can be affected by practice, outdated norms, brief or group testing, uneven subtest profiles, language and culture, and co-occurring problems with communication, language, movement or the senses. A profile of strengths and weaknesses from neuropsychological testing is more informative than a single IQ score. An IQ score alone cannot capture reasoning in real life or practical skill, so a person scoring somewhat above the usual range may still, because of marked adaptive problems in social judgment or other areas, function like people with lower scores.
Criterion B concerns how far a person lives up to the standards their community sets for independence and social responsibility, compared with others of similar age and background. Adaptive functioning covers three domains. The conceptual (academic) domain covers memory, language, reading, writing, mathematical reasoning, gaining practical knowledge, problem solving, judgment in new situations and similar abilities. The social domain covers awareness of what others think, feel and experience, empathy, skills of interpersonal communication, the ability to make friends, and judgment in social matters. The practical domain covers learning and managing oneself across settings, including personal care, duties at work, handling money, leisure, managing one's own behavior, and organizing school and work tasks. Adaptive functioning is shaped by intellectual capacity, schooling, motivation, upbringing, personality, work opportunity, culture and other medical or mental conditions. It is assessed by clinical evaluation and by standardized measures completed with people who know the person well (for example parents, teachers or carers) and with the person where possible.
Criterion B is satisfied once any one of the three adaptive domains, conceptual, social or practical, is so impaired that the person needs continuing support to cope at home, at school, at work and in the community.
Criterion C, onset during development, means that the intellectual and adaptive shortfalls are recognized in childhood or adolescence.
A full evaluation also looks for genetic and non-genetic causes, associated medical conditions (for example cerebral palsy or seizures), and other mental, emotional and behavioral disorders that occur alongside.

Associated Features Supporting Diagnosis
========================================
The condition is heterogeneous and has many causes. There may be associated difficulty with social judgment, with judging risk, with managing one's own behavior, emotions or relationships, or with motivation at school or at work. Poor awareness of risk and danger can raise the rate of accidental injury. Limited communication skills can make disruptive and aggressive behavior more likely. Gullibility, meaning naivety in social situations and being easily led, is common, and together with poor awareness of risk it can lead to exploitation and victimization. People may also become distressed about their own intellectual limits.
